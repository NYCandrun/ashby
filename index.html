A three-minute briefing

Blind Briefing.

You are Head of Talent at Meridian, a 300-person software company hiring hard. You have two AI recruiting assistants, and both answer every question you ask. Your job is to pick the answer you would actually act on.

The setup

Rounds
7 questions
Each round
Read both answers, pick one
Feedback
None until the end

Round 1 of 7

Both assistants answered. Pick the one you would act on.

The reveal

The two assistants ran the same model. Both answered with numbers, sources, and confidence, and nothing on the screen separated them. One saw all of your data. One saw pieces of it.

Every wrong answer in this game leads to an action that works. The salary bands go up. The email sends. The managers clear their queue. Nothing errors and nothing warns, and every result looks like progress. The cost shows up weeks later, somewhere else — a search restarts, a budget is overspent, a strong candidate signs elsewhere — and nothing connects it back to the advice. That's what partial data does to a process. It doesn't break it. It makes it fail silently.

Fragmented stack

Unified system

One system, one data model, everything joined.

Round by round

Meridian is fictional. The failure mode is not: every wrong answer here is what AI produces when a real capability is missing — live pipeline data, candidate history, interview load, feedback, and outcomes, joined in one place.
